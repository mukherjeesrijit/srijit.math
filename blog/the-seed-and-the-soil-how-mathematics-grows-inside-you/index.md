# The Seed and the Soil: How Mathematics Grows Inside You

A student wrote to me from far away.

They had fallen in love with mathematics. Truly fallen: the kind of love that makes you forget to eat, forget the time, forget everything else. And because there was no map, they walked into it alone. They studied everything and nothing. They neglected every other subject. And they failed their final exams.

Then came the part that hurt the most. They wrote:

> *"I understand concepts when I study, but when it matters, my mind goes blank. I feel completely empty."*

If you have ever felt this, I want you to read slowly. Because what I wrote back to them, I am now writing to you.

## It begins with a quiet decision

Before any book, before any roadmap, there is one thing.

You decide that you want to be great at mathematics.

Not "good enough". Not "pass the exam". *Great.*

The ancient Greeks had a word for this: **Arete**. Excellence. The idea that something inside you is reaching toward the best version of itself. Arete is not given to you by a teacher. It comes from within. And on the days when you don't feel like opening the book, and those days will come, it is **Arete** that lets you take one small step anyway.

Notice something. You are reading this. You are looking for something more. That search is already the sign. The person who asks "how do I get better?" is already becoming better.

## Give your desire a shape

A desire without a shape drifts.

So bring it into the light. Ask yourself, honestly:

*What exactly do I want to learn? And how much time do I have?*

An exam in four months. A summer break. One book before the year ends. It doesn't matter which. What matters is that you can say it out loud. The moment your desire becomes a goal with a timeline, your mind stops wandering and starts planning. You begin to carve your own path.

This question cannot be skipped. Everything else rests on it.

## Why your mind goes blank

When you understand something at your desk and lose it in the exam hall, the problem is usually not your memory.

It is fear.

Fear is a belief: *I am not good enough. I will fail again.* And a belief can only be answered by another belief. That is why **Arete** comes first. When you believe in your own capacity to grow, fear loosens its grip. And as it loosens, what you know comes back to you.

## Your knowledge is a family tree

Now, gently, look at what you know.

Mathematics is a family tree. A few fundamental ideas sit at the roots. Everything else is born from them, combined, recombined, grown into new branches. When a branch feels weak, the problem is almost always further down, closer to the roots.

So your next task is to find exactly where.

Here is a simple way. Sit with an AI and ask it to *question you* on a topic, starting from the most basic ideas. Not to explain. To ask. Answer honestly. Where you hesitate, there is your gap. Write it down in a journal. Each evening, read what you wrote.

Keep going until you find **the smallest of the smallest** thing you need to master. Small enough to hold in one hand.

Then decide how much time you will give it.

## How a subject truly enters you

A subject is learned through three things: **practice, active learning, and testing yourself.**

Take one good book, one with both theory and problems. Take a piece of paper. Take a pen.

Read slowly. Take notes. When something excites you, write it down; that excitement is your mind telling you something matters. Draw pictures of the ideas. Connect one idea to another. Let the AI help you imagine new visual pictures if you need them, but then draw them yourself, by hand, on paper.

There is something your hand knows that your eyes do not. When you write, the motion carries the idea deeper than reading ever can.

And remember how mathematics itself grows:

**Arithmetic → examples → algebra → geometry.**

First numbers. Then many examples with numbers. Then algebra, which is simply those examples, abstracted. Then geometry, where you *see* what the algebra was saying.

So when something won't make sense, go backward. Ask for an example in plain numbers. Ask for a picture. Then come forward again.

When you finish a section, close the book and try the solved examples on your own. If you can't, notice how far you got. Notice exactly where you stopped.

And then, this is important, **stay stuck.**

Being stuck is not failure. Being stuck is the moment your mind is building something new.

## How you know you understand

Here is a beautiful sign.

You begin to *predict* the next idea before the book shows it to you. A question rises in your mind, and two pages later, the author asks the same question.

That is understanding. You are no longer receiving information. You are playing with it.

Then solve the problems at the end of the chapter. Draw. Map. Write down your ideas. Look for patterns. Make your own guesses. Try to prove them. Chase the little unsolved questions that live inside you.

And as you keep doing this, quietly, day after day, something inside you becomes richer. Your inner picture of the ideas grows deeper, more connected, more alive.

## The seed and the soil

Then comes the part almost everyone forgets.

You give it time.

Every page you read, every picture you draw, every problem you struggle with is a seed. And your subconscious is the soil.

Seeds don't grow because you stare at them. They grow because the soil is right: good food, deep sleep, less stress, a little patience. You keep planting, and you let the soil do its work.

One morning you will open a problem that defeated you a week ago, and you will simply *see* it. You won't know when it happened. That is the subconscious. That is the seed becoming a tree.

And if you ever can't move forward, don't force it. Pick a different topic and plant there. Or rest. Rest is not the opposite of learning. Rest is where learning settles.

## You already have the map

People often ask me for a roadmap.

Here is the truth: **the best roadmaps are already written. They are called books.** Your goal is not to find a perfect path. Your goal is to finish one good book, or one good set of resources, completely.

Everything you need comes down to five things:

1. **Arete:** the belief, from within, that you can be excellent.
2. **A roadmap:** a book or resource that already exists.
3. **A way of learning:** practice, active learning, testing yourself.
4. **Execution:** actually doing it, today.
5. **Reflection:** honestly noticing what works, and what doesn't.

## If you feel lost, start here

Choose the simplest topic you want to learn: the one you have been trying to understand for the longest time.

That topic carries the most emotion. That is exactly why it is the right place to begin.

If it feels too big, ask: *what is the smallest, most fundamental idea inside it?* Master only that. When it works for one small topic, go one level up. Then one more.

Belief carries you on the days you want to give up. Strategy shows you the way. Execution moves you forward.

And slowly, almost without noticing, mathematics stops being something you study, and becomes something that grows in you.
