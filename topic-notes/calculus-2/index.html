Calculus II

1. Limits and continuity in several variables

lim(x,y)→(a,b)​f(x,y)=L means ∣f−L∣<ϵ whenever 0<∥(x,y)−(a,b)∥<δ. The limit must be the same along every path.

  • To show a limit does not exist: find two paths with different limits, e.g. lines y=mx and curves y=x2, x=y2. For x2+y4xy2​ the lines give 0 but x=y2 gives 21​.
  • To show a limit exists: bound ∣f−L∣ by something that tends to 0. Polar coordinates help: x=rcosθ, y=rsinθ, and show ∣f−L∣≤g(r)→0 uniformly in θ.
  • Useful bounds: ∣x∣,∣y∣≤x2+y2​; ∣xy∣≤2x2+y2​; x2+y2x4+y4​≤x2+y2.
  • Homogeneity: if f is a ratio of homogeneous polynomials of degrees p (top) and q (bottom) with the bottom vanishing only at 0, then f→0 when p>q, and the limit usually fails when p=q.

2. Partial derivatives and differentiability

  • fx​(a,b)=limh→0​hf(a+h,b)−f(a,b)​, computed from the definition at special points.
  • Directional derivative: Du​f(a)=limt→0​tf(a+tu)−f(a)​; for differentiable f, Du​f=∇f⋅u.
  • Differentiable at a: there is a linear L with ∥h∥f(a+h)−f(a)−L(h)​→0; then L(h)=∇f(a)⋅h.
  • Test at the origin: compute fx​,fy​ from the definition, then check whether h2+k2​f(h,k)−f(0,0)−fx​h−fy​k​→0.

Implications

  • Continuous partial derivatives near a ⇒ differentiable at a ⇒ continuous at a and all directional derivatives exist.
  • None of these reverse: partial derivatives (even all directional derivatives) can exist at a point where f is not continuous.

Chain rule. If w=f(u,v), u=u(x,y), v=v(x,y), then wx​=fu​ux​+fv​vx​.

Jacobian. For F:Rn→Rn, J=(∂xj​∂Fi​​); detJ measures local area/volume scaling.

Taylor expansion (second order):

f(a+h,b+k)≈f+hfx​+kfy​+21​(h2fxx​+2hkfxy​+k2fyy​).

Clairaut: fxy​=fyx​ when these are continuous.

3. Maxima and minima

  • Critical points: ∇f=0.
  • Second derivative test with D=fxx​fyy​−fxy2​ at a critical point:
    • D>0, fxx​>0: local minimum; D>0, fxx​<0: local maximum;
    • D<0: saddle point;
    • D=0: inconclusive. Then look along lines or curves, e.g. f(t,0), f(t,−t), or x3−3xy2 (a "monkey saddle").
  • On a closed bounded region: the extremes occur at interior critical points or on the boundary; parametrise the boundary and reduce to one variable, then compare all candidates.
  • Lagrange multipliers: to optimise f subject to g=c, solve ∇f=λ∇g, g=c. The closest point of a sphere to a point lies on the line through the centre.
  • AM–GM often gives extrema faster: e.g. xy2z3 with x+y+z fixed, or the triangle of largest area with fixed perimeter (equilateral, by Heron and AM–GM).

4. Double and triple integrals

  • Fubini: for continuous f on a rectangle, ∬f=∫(∫fdy)dx=∫(∫fdx)dy.
  • Changing the order: sketch the region, then describe it the other way. Use it when the inner integral has no elementary antiderivative, e.g. ∫01​∫x​1​1+y3dydx​ or ∫01​∫y21​x​ex​dxdy.
  • Polar coordinates: dxdy=rdrdθ. Good for discs, sectors and x2+y2.
  • Cylindrical: dV=rdrdθdz. Spherical: dV=ρ2sinϕdρdϕdθ.
  • Change of variables: ∬f(x,y)dxdy=∬f(x(u,v),y(u,v))∣detJ∣dudv. Parallelograms become rectangles under a linear change.
  • Symmetry: an odd integrand over a symmetric region integrates to 0; on 0≤x≤y≤z≤1 use the 3! orderings of the cube.
  • Gaussian integrals: ∬e−(x2+y2)=π; quadratic forms ∬e−(ax2+2bxy+cy2)=ac−b2​π​.
