Calculus II

1. Limits and continuity in several variables

$\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)=L$ means $|f-L|<\epsilon$ whenever $0<\|(x,y)-(a,b)\|<\delta$. The limit must be the same along every path.

  • To show a limit does not exist: find two paths with different limits, e.g. lines $y=mx$ and curves $y=x^2$, $x=y^2$. For $\frac{xy^2}{x^2+y^4}$ the lines give $0$ but $x=y^2$ gives $\frac12$.
  • To show a limit exists: bound $|f-L|$ by something that tends to $0$. Polar coordinates help: $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, and show $|f-L|\le g(r)\to0$ uniformly in $\theta$.
  • Useful bounds: $|x|,|y|\le\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$; $|xy|\le\frac{x^2+y^2}2$; $\frac{x^4+y^4}{x^2+y^2}\le x^2+y^2$.
  • Homogeneity: if $f$ is a ratio of homogeneous polynomials of degrees $p$ (top) and $q$ (bottom) with the bottom vanishing only at $0$, then $f\to0$ when $p>q$, and the limit usually fails when $p=q$.

2. Partial derivatives and differentiability

  • $f_x(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}h$, computed from the definition at special points.
  • Directional derivative: $D_uf(a)=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(a+tu)-f(a)}t$; for differentiable $f$, $D_uf=\nabla f\cdot u$.
  • Differentiable at $a$: there is a linear $L$ with $\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)-L(h)}{\|h\|}\to0$; then $L(h)=\nabla f(a)\cdot h$.
  • Test at the origin: compute $f_x,f_y$ from the definition, then check whether $\frac{f(h,k)-f(0,0)-f_xh-f_yk}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2}}\to0$.

Implications

  • Continuous partial derivatives near $a$ $\Rightarrow$ differentiable at $a$ $\Rightarrow$ continuous at $a$ and all directional derivatives exist.
  • None of these reverse: partial derivatives (even all directional derivatives) can exist at a point where $f$ is not continuous.

Chain rule. If $w=f(u,v)$, $u=u(x,y)$, $v=v(x,y)$, then $w_x=f_uu_x+f_vv_x$.

Jacobian. For $F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n$, $J=\left(\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_j}\right)$; $\det J$ measures local area/volume scaling.

Taylor expansion (second order):

$$f(a+h,b+k)\approx f+hf_x+kf_y+\tfrac12\left(h^2f_{xx}+2hkf_{xy}+k^2f_{yy}\right).$$

Clairaut: $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$ when these are continuous.

3. Maxima and minima

  • Critical points: $\nabla f=0$.
  • Second derivative test with $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$ at a critical point:
    • $D>0$, $f_{xx}>0$: local minimum; $D>0$, $f_{xx}<0$: local maximum;
    • $D<0$: saddle point;
    • $D=0$: inconclusive. Then look along lines or curves, e.g. $f(t,0)$, $f(t,-t)$, or $x^3-3xy^2$ (a "monkey saddle").
  • On a closed bounded region: the extremes occur at interior critical points or on the boundary; parametrise the boundary and reduce to one variable, then compare all candidates.
  • Lagrange multipliers: to optimise $f$ subject to $g=c$, solve $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$, $g=c$. The closest point of a sphere to a point lies on the line through the centre.
  • AM–GM often gives extrema faster: e.g. $xy^2z^3$ with $x+y+z$ fixed, or the triangle of largest area with fixed perimeter (equilateral, by Heron and AM–GM).

4. Double and triple integrals

  • Fubini: for continuous $f$ on a rectangle, $\iint f=\int\!\left(\int f\,dy\right)dx=\int\!\left(\int f\,dx\right)dy$.
  • Changing the order: sketch the region, then describe it the other way. Use it when the inner integral has no elementary antiderivative, e.g. $\int_0^1\!\int_{\sqrt x}^1\frac{dy\,dx}{1+y^3}$ or $\int_0^1\!\int_{y^2}^1\frac{e^x}{\sqrt x}\,dx\,dy$.
  • Polar coordinates: $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$. Good for discs, sectors and $x^2+y^2$.
  • Cylindrical: $dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$. Spherical: $dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$.
  • Change of variables: $\iint f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\iint f(x(u,v),y(u,v))\,|\det J|\,du\,dv$. Parallelograms become rectangles under a linear change.
  • Symmetry: an odd integrand over a symmetric region integrates to $0$; on $0\le x\le y\le z\le1$ use the $3!$ orderings of the cube.
  • Gaussian integrals: $\iint e^{-(x^2+y^2)}=\pi$; quadratic forms $\iint e^{-(ax^2+2bxy+cy^2)}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{ac-b^2}}$.
  • Volumes: $\iint_R(\text{top}-\text{bottom})\,dA$. Volume under $z=f(x,y)$ over $R$ is $\iint_Rf\,dA$.
  • Differentiating integrals: $\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^x\!\int_0^xf(u,v)\,du\,dv=\int_0^x f(x,v)\,dv+\int_0^x f(u,x)\,du$.
