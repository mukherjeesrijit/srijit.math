Linear Algebra

1. Vector spaces

A vector space over R is a set V with addition and scalar multiplication satisfying:

  • addition is commutative and associative, there is a zero vector 0, and each v has an additive inverse −v;
  • a(u+v)=au+av, (a+b)v=av+bv, (ab)v=a(bv), and 1⋅v=v.

Standard examples

  • Rn.
  • Mm×n​(R), the m×n real matrices, which has dimension mn.
  • Pn​, the real polynomials of degree ≤n, which has dimension n+1.
  • Continuous functions on an interval (infinite-dimensional).

2. Subspaces

W⊆V is a subspace if 0∈W and au+bv∈W for all u,v∈W and scalars a,b.

Standard subspaces

  • The kernel and image of a linear map.
  • The span of any set of vectors.
  • Intersections of subspaces. (A union is generally not a subspace.)
  • The sum U+W={u+w}.
  • The orthogonal complement W⊥.

Dimension formula: dim(U+W)=dimU+dimW−dim(U∩W).

Direct sum: V=U⊕W means V=U+W and U∩W={0}. Then every v is written uniquely as u+w, and dimV=dimU+dimW.

Counting dimensions of matrix subspaces

  • Symmetric n×n matrices: 2n(n+1)​.
  • Skew-symmetric n×n matrices (At=−A): 2n(n−1)​. The diagonal must be zero and the upper triangle determines the rest.
  • Trace-zero n×n matrices: n2−1. The trace is a nonzero linear functional, and the kernel of a nonzero functional has codimension 1.
  • Block-diagonal matrices with independent blocks: the dimensions add.

3. Span, independence, basis, dimension

  • span(S) is the set of all finite linear combinations of vectors in S.
  • v1​,…,vk​ are linearly independent if ∑ci​vi​=0 forces all ci​=0.
  • A basis is a linearly independent spanning set. Every v has a unique expression in a basis.
  • Steinitz exchange: in a space spanned by m vectors, any independent set has at most m vectors. Hence all bases have the same size, called dimV.
  • In an n-dimensional space, any n independent vectors form a basis, and any n spanning vectors form a basis.
  • Every independent set extends to a basis, and every spanning set contains a basis.

4. Inner products and orthogonality

An inner product ⟨⋅,⋅⟩ on a real vector space is symmetric, linear in each slot, and positive definite (⟨x,x⟩>0 for x=0). On Rn the standard one is ⟨x,y⟩=xty.

  • Norm: ∥x∥=⟨x,x⟩​, with ∥cx∥=∣c∣∥x∥.
  • Cauchy–Schwarz: ∣⟨x,y⟩∣≤∥x∥∥y∥.
  • Triangle inequality: ∥x+y∥≤∥x∥+∥y∥.
  • Orthogonal: x⊥y when ⟨x,y⟩=0. Orthogonal nonzero vectors are independent.
  • Orthogonal complement: W⊥={x:⟨x,w⟩=0 ∀w∈W}. In Rn:
    • dimW+dimW⊥=n;
    • Rn=W⊕W⊥;
    • (W⊥)⊥=W.
  • Gram–Schmidt turns any basis into an orthonormal basis.
  • For a matrix A: (ColA)⊥=kerAt and (RowA)⊥=kerA.

5. Linear maps

T:V→W is linear if T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v).

  • kerT={v:Tv=0} is a subspace of V. ImT={Tv} is a subspace of W.
  • T is one-one ⟺kerT={0}.
  • T is onto ⟺ImT=W.
  • A linear map is determined by its values on a basis, and these values can be chosen freely.
  • Compositions and sums of linear maps are linear.
  • T is an isomorphism (invertible) ⟺ it is one-one and onto. Spaces of equal finite dimension are isomorphic. For dimV=dimW<∞, one-one ⟺ onto.

Maps and norms: if ∥Tx∥=c∥x∥ for all unit x with c>0, then by homogeneity ∥Tx∥=c∥x∥ for all x. Hence kerT=0 and T is injective. Such a map is c times an isometry, and it requires n≤m. It is not necessarily onto, and it is unbounded in the sense that ∥Tx∥ grows with ∥x∥.

6. Matrices and change of basis

  • For bases B of V and C of W, the matrix [T]BC​ has columns [Tbj​]C​. Then [Tv]C​=[T]BC​[v]B​.
  • Matrix multiplication corresponds to composition of maps.
  • Change of basis for an operator: if P is the matrix whose columns are the new basis vectors written in the old basis, then [T]new​=P−1[T]old​P.

Invertibility. For an n×n matrix, the following are equivalent:

  • A is invertible.
  • detA=0.
  • RankA=n.
  • kerA={0}.
  • The columns (or rows) are independent.
  • 0 is not an eigenvalue.
  • Ax=b has a unique solution for every b.

7. Rank, kernel, and rank–nullity

Rank. RankA=dimColA, the dimension of the column space. It equals the dimension of the row space, so RankA=RankAt. It also equals the number of pivots after row reduction.

Rank–nullity theorem. For T:V→W with V finite-dimensional,

dimV=dimkerT+dimImT.

Idea of proof: extend a basis of kerT to a basis of V. The images of the added vectors form a basis of ImT.

Rank inequalities

  • RankA≤min(m,n) for an m×n matrix.
  • Rank(AB)≤min(RankA,RankB).
  • Rank(A+B)≤RankA+RankB.
  • RankA≤ the number of nonzero rows, and ≤ the number of nonzero columns.
  • Multiplying by invertible matrices on either side doesn't change rank.

Rank-one matrices. RankA=1⟺A=uvt with u,v=0. Then the nonzero entries are aij​=ui​vj​, and the count of nonzero entries is (number of nonzero ui​) × (number of nonzero vj​). In a 4×4 matrix, a rank-one matrix therefore has pq nonzero entries with p,q∈{1,2,3,4}, and 5 is not such a product.

Left kernel. {y:ytA=0} has dimension m−RankA. A nonzero B with BA=0 exists exactly when RankA<m, because each row of B must lie in the left kernel. Any matrix with a zero row has rank less than its number of rows.

8. Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, characteristic polynomial

  • λ is an eigenvalue of A if Av=λv for some v=0 (an eigenvector). Equivalently, det(A−λI)=0.
  • The eigenspace Eλ​=ker(A−λI) has dimension called the geometric multiplicity.
  • The characteristic polynomial pA​(x)=det(xI−A) has degree n. Its coefficients involve the trace and determinant:
    • the coefficient of xn−1 is −trA;
    • the constant term is (−1)ndetA.
  • The algebraic multiplicity of λ is its multiplicity as a root of pA​.
  • Always: 1≤geometric≤algebraic.
  • Sums and products: the eigenvalues (with multiplicity) add up to trA and multiply to detA.
  • Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent.
  • If λ is an eigenvalue of A, then f(λ) is an eigenvalue of f(A) for any polynomial f. For invertible A, λ−1 is an eigenvalue of A−1.
  • A triangular matrix has its diagonal entries as eigenvalues.

Cayley–Hamilton theorem. pA​(A)=0.

Minimal polynomial. mA​ is the monic polynomial of least degree with mA​(A)=0. It divides every polynomial that annihilates A (in particular pA​), and it has the same roots as pA​.

Inverse via Cayley–Hamilton. If pA​(x)=x3+c2​x2+c1​x+c0​ with c0​=0, then

A3+c2​A2+c1​A=−c0​I ⇒ A−1=−c0​1​(A2+c2​A+c1​I).

For eigenvalues 1,2,3: pA​(x)=x3−6x2+11x−6, so A−1=61​(A2−6A+11I).

9. Similarity

A∼B if B=P−1AP for some invertible P, meaning the two matrices represent the same operator in different bases. Similarity is an equivalence relation.

Preserved by similarity (invariants):

  • rank and nullity;
  • determinant and trace;
  • invertibility;
  • characteristic polynomial, minimal polynomial, eigenvalues with multiplicities;
  • dimker(A−λI)j for every λ and j.

Not preserved as sets: the kernel, image and eigenvectors themselves. They are moved by P: kerB=P−1(kerA). Only their dimensions agree.

Two matrices with the same characteristic polynomial need not be similar (for example 0 and (00​10​)).

10. Diagonalizability

A is diagonalizable if A=PDP−1 with D diagonal.

Equivalent conditions

  • Rn has a basis of eigenvectors of A.
  • The geometric multiplicity equals the algebraic multiplicity for every eigenvalue, and pA​ splits into linear factors over the field.
  • The minimal polynomial is a product of distinct linear factors.

Sufficient conditions: n distinct eigenvalues, or A real symmetric (spectral theorem).

Spectral theorem. A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues, eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and A=QΛQt with Q orthogonal and Λ diagonal.

Failure of diagonalizability happens when some geometric multiplicity is strictly smaller than the algebraic one, which leads to Jordan form.

11. Nilpotent operators and Jordan form

Nilpotent. Tk=0 for some k. Then:

  • the only eigenvalue is 0 (if Tv=λv, then 0=Tkv=λkv);
  • pT​(x)=xn and the minimal polynomial is xk for the least such k;
  • Tn=0 always, where n=dimV.

Nilpotent and diagonalizable implies zero. If T were diagonalizable, it would be similar to a diagonal matrix with only zeros, so T=0. Hence a nonzero nilpotent operator is never diagonalizable.

Case dimV=2, T2=0, T=0. Here ImT=kerT is a line. For v∈/kerT, the pair {v,Tv} is a basis, and T∼(01​00​). This is the only nonzero nilpotent type in R2.

Jordan block. Jk​(λ) is the k×k matrix with λ on the diagonal and 1 on the superdiagonal (or subdiagonal).

Jordan canonical form. If pA​ splits over the field, A is similar to a block-diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks, unique up to reordering the blocks. For λ:

  • the number of blocks with eigenvalue λ equals dimker(A−λI);
  • the total size of those blocks equals the algebraic multiplicity;
  • the largest block size equals the multiplicity of λ as a root of mA​;
  • the numbers dimker(A−λI)j determine all block sizes.

Similarity criterion. Two matrices with split characteristic polynomials are similar ⟺ they have the same Jordan form. In particular, similar operators have equal dimkerTj for every j.

Example: differentiation on P5​. The operator S=dxd​ on polynomials of degree ≤5 (dimension 6) sends x5→x4→⋯→x→1→0.

  • It is one Jordan block J6​(0).
  • dimkerS=1 and RankS=5.
  • T=S2 has dimkerT=2 (it kills 1 and x), so T splits into two Jordan blocks of size 3 and is not similar to S.
  • S∘T=T∘S=S3, since powers of one operator commute. It is nilpotent, so 0 is its only eigenvalue.
