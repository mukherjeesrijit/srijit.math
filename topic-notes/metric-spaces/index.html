Metric Spaces & Topology

1. Metrics, open and closed sets

A metric d on X satisfies d(x,y)≥0 with equality iff x=y, symmetry, and the triangle inequality d(x,z)≤d(x,y)+d(y,z).

  • Examples: Euclidean, ℓ1, ℓ∞ on Rn; the discrete metric; ∣ex−ey∣ on R; ∣AΔB∣ on finite sets; the sup metric sup∣f−g∣ on C[a,b]; ∣m1​−n1​∣ on N.
  • Not metrics: functions failing d(x,x)=0 (∣V∩W∣), symmetry (∣V∖W∣), or the triangle inequality (largest side greater than the sum of the other two).
  • Ultrametric (d(x,y)≤max{d(x,z),d(y,z)}): every triangle is isosceles with the two longest sides equal; two balls of the same radius are equal or disjoint.
  • Open ball B(x,r)={y:d(x,y)<r}. U is open if it contains a ball around each of its points; F is closed if its complement is open, equivalently it contains all its limit points.
  • Arbitrary unions and finite intersections of open sets are open. In a finite metric space every set is open (the topology is discrete), so there are 2n open sets.
  • Interior, closure, boundary: A=A∪{limit points}; x∈A⟺d(x,A)=0. A is dense if A=X.
  • Distance to a set: d(x,A)=infa∈A​d(x,a) is 1-Lipschitz. Disjoint sets can be at distance 0 (even disjoint closed sets).
  • Open sets in R are countable disjoint unions of open intervals. Proper subspaces of Rn are closed, never open.
  • Small intervals around every rational, ⋃(qn​−n21​,qn​+n21​), form a dense open set that is not all of R (total length <4).

2. Continuity

  • f:X→Y is continuous iff preimages of open sets are open iff xn​→x implies f(xn​)→f(x).
  • If f(xn​) converges whenever xn​ converges, then f is continuous (interleave the sequence with the constant x).
  • Continuous maps preserve convergent sequences, convergent subsequences, compactness and connectedness, but not Cauchy sequences (x1​ on (0,1)).
  • Uniform continuity in metric spaces: one δ for all points. Lipschitz maps are uniformly continuous; x↦d(x,A) is 1-Lipschitz.
  • Extension: a uniformly continuous map from a dense subset into a complete space extends uniquely to the whole space; merely continuous maps need not extend (sinx1​).
  • Isometries d(f(x),f(y))=d(x,y) are injective and continuous. Isometries between subsets of Rn extend to rigid motions; an isometry of R-subsets is x↦±x+c.
  • Embedding in Rn: not every finite metric space embeds isometrically (a centre at distance 1 from three points pairwise at distance 2).
  • Homeomorphism: a continuous bijection with continuous inverse. Invariants: compactness, connectedness, number of components, removing points.
  • Functions into a discrete space like {0,1} are continuous iff each level set is open; on Q there are uncountably many (cut at any irrational).

3. Compactness

  • Definitions (equivalent in metric spaces): every open cover has a finite subcover; every sequence has a convergent subsequence (sequential compactness); complete and totally bounded.
  • Heine–Borel: in Rn, compact ⟺ closed and bounded. A conic f(x,y)=1 is compact iff it is an ellipse (positive definite quadratic part).
  • Closed subsets of compact sets are compact; compact subsets of metric spaces are closed and bounded.
  • Continuous images of compact sets are compact: a continuous real function on a compact set is bounded, attains its max and min, and is uniformly continuous.
  • Compact to Hausdorff: a continuous bijection from a compact space onto a metric space is a homeomorphism; a continuous injection is a homeomorphism onto its image.
  • Isometries of compact spaces into themselves are onto.
  • Not compact: Q∩[0,1] (a sequence converging to an irrational); the closed unit ball of C[0,1]; the set of 21​-Hölder functions (contains all constants).
  • Arzelà–Ascoli: a subset of C[a,b] is relatively compact iff it is bounded and equicontinuous.
  • Preimages: if ∣f′∣≥1 on R, then f−1 of a compact set is compact (closed and bounded).
  • N∪{∞} with d(m,n)=∣m1​−n1​∣ is compact (it is {n1​}∪{0}).

4. Completeness

  • Complete: every Cauchy sequence converges. Rn, closed subsets of complete spaces, C[a,b] with the sup metric: complete. Q, (0,1), (R,∣ex−ey∣): not complete.
  • A complete subspace of a metric space is closed. If X∖{x} is complete, x is isolated.
  • Banach fixed point: a contraction d(f(x),f(y))≤cd(x,y) with c<1 on a nonempty complete space has a unique fixed point, the limit of x,f(x),f2(x),…. It suffices that some iterate fk is a contraction.
  • Baire category theorem: a complete metric space is not a countable union of nowhere dense closed sets. Hence a countable complete metric space has isolated points (in fact infinitely many if it is infinite), and R is uncountable.
  • Completion: every metric space sits densely in a complete one (Q⊂R).

5. Connectedness

  • Connected: not a union of two disjoint nonempty open sets. In R the connected sets are exactly the intervals.
  • Path connected ⇒ connected. The converse fails: the topologist's sine curve {(x,sinx1​):x>0} together with points of {0}×[−1,1] is connected but not path connected. Open connected subsets of Rn are path connected.
  • Continuous images of connected sets are connected (IVT is the case of R). A connected metric space with two points is uncountable (x↦d(a,x) hits an interval).
  • Closure: if A is connected and A⊆B⊆A, then B is connected.
  • Components: maximal connected subsets; they are closed. Count them with continuous invariants: {(b,c):b2=4c} has two; {f:∫f=0} in C[0,1] has two; {A:AAt=M} for positive definite M is a copy of O(2), with two.
  • Graphs: the graph of a continuous function on an interval is connected (a solution set like (x3+1)3=(y5+1)5 is such a graph).
  • Removing sets: Rn minus a countable union of lines (n≥3), or Cn minus a complex hypersurface, stays path connected; R minus a point does not.
  • Showing non-homeomorphic: count components after removing a point (R≅R2; a circle is not homeomorphic to an interval). There is no continuous bijection R→[0,1].
