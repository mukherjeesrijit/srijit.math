Metric Spaces & Topology

1. Metrics, open and closed sets

A metric $d$ on $X$ satisfies $d(x,y)\ge0$ with equality iff $x=y$, symmetry, and the triangle inequality $d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)$.

  • Examples: Euclidean, $\ell^1$, $\ell^\infty$ on $\mathbb R^n$; the discrete metric; $|e^x-e^y|$ on $\mathbb R$; $|A\,\Delta\,B|$ on finite sets; the sup metric $\sup|f-g|$ on $C[a,b]$; $|\frac1m-\frac1n|$ on $\mathbb N$.
  • Not metrics: functions failing $d(x,x)=0$ ($|V\cap W|$), symmetry ($|V\setminus W|$), or the triangle inequality (largest side greater than the sum of the other two).
  • Ultrametric ($d(x,y)\le\max\{d(x,z),d(y,z)\}$): every triangle is isosceles with the two longest sides equal; two balls of the same radius are equal or disjoint.
  • Open ball $B(x,r)=\{y:d(x,y)<r\}$. $U$ is open if it contains a ball around each of its points; $F$ is closed if its complement is open, equivalently it contains all its limit points.
  • Arbitrary unions and finite intersections of open sets are open. In a finite metric space every set is open (the topology is discrete), so there are $2^n$ open sets.
  • Interior, closure, boundary: $\overline A=A\cup\{\text{limit points}\}$; $x\in\overline A\iff d(x,A)=0$. $A$ is dense if $\overline A=X$.
  • Distance to a set: $d(x,A)=\inf_{a\in A}d(x,a)$ is $1$-Lipschitz. Disjoint sets can be at distance $0$ (even disjoint closed sets).
  • Open sets in $\mathbb R$ are countable disjoint unions of open intervals. Proper subspaces of $\mathbb R^n$ are closed, never open.
  • Small intervals around every rational, $\bigcup(q_n-\frac1{n^2},q_n+\frac1{n^2})$, form a dense open set that is not all of $\mathbb R$ (total length $<4$).

2. Continuity

  • $f:X\to Y$ is continuous iff preimages of open sets are open iff $x_n\to x$ implies $f(x_n)\to f(x)$.
  • If $f(x_n)$ converges whenever $x_n$ converges, then $f$ is continuous (interleave the sequence with the constant $x$).
  • Continuous maps preserve convergent sequences, convergent subsequences, compactness and connectedness, but not Cauchy sequences ($\frac1x$ on $(0,1)$).
  • Uniform continuity in metric spaces: one $\delta$ for all points. Lipschitz maps are uniformly continuous; $x\mapsto d(x,A)$ is $1$-Lipschitz.
  • Extension: a uniformly continuous map from a dense subset into a complete space extends uniquely to the whole space; merely continuous maps need not extend ($\sin\frac1x$).
  • Isometries $d(f(x),f(y))=d(x,y)$ are injective and continuous. Isometries between subsets of $\mathbb R^n$ extend to rigid motions; an isometry of $\mathbb R$-subsets is $x\mapsto\pm x+c$.
  • Embedding in $\mathbb R^n$: not every finite metric space embeds isometrically (a centre at distance $1$ from three points pairwise at distance $2$).
  • Homeomorphism: a continuous bijection with continuous inverse. Invariants: compactness, connectedness, number of components, removing points.
  • Functions into a discrete space like $\{0,1\}$ are continuous iff each level set is open; on $\mathbb Q$ there are uncountably many (cut at any irrational).

3. Compactness

  • Definitions (equivalent in metric spaces): every open cover has a finite subcover; every sequence has a convergent subsequence (sequential compactness); complete and totally bounded.
  • Heine–Borel: in $\mathbb R^n$, compact $\iff$ closed and bounded. A conic $f(x,y)=1$ is compact iff it is an ellipse (positive definite quadratic part).
  • Closed subsets of compact sets are compact; compact subsets of metric spaces are closed and bounded.
  • Continuous images of compact sets are compact: a continuous real function on a compact set is bounded, attains its max and min, and is uniformly continuous.
  • Compact to Hausdorff: a continuous bijection from a compact space onto a metric space is a homeomorphism; a continuous injection is a homeomorphism onto its image.
  • Isometries of compact spaces into themselves are onto.
  • Not compact: $\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]$ (a sequence converging to an irrational); the closed unit ball of $C[0,1]$; the set of $\frac12$-Hölder functions (contains all constants).
  • Arzelà–Ascoli: a subset of $C[a,b]$ is relatively compact iff it is bounded and equicontinuous.
  • Preimages: if $|f'|\ge1$ on $\mathbb R$, then $f^{-1}$ of a compact set is compact (closed and bounded).
  • $\mathbb N\cup\{\infty\}$ with $d(m,n)=|\frac1m-\frac1n|$ is compact (it is $\{\frac1n\}\cup\{0\}$).

4. Completeness

  • Complete: every Cauchy sequence converges. $\mathbb R^n$, closed subsets of complete spaces, $C[a,b]$ with the sup metric: complete. $\mathbb Q$, $(0,1)$, $(\mathbb R,|e^x-e^y|)$: not complete.
  • A complete subspace of a metric space is closed. If $X\setminus\{x\}$ is complete, $x$ is isolated.
  • Banach fixed point: a contraction $d(f(x),f(y))\le c\,d(x,y)$ with $c<1$ on a nonempty complete space has a unique fixed point, the limit of $x,f(x),f^2(x),\dots$. It suffices that some iterate $f^k$ is a contraction.
  • Baire category theorem: a complete metric space is not a countable union of nowhere dense closed sets. Hence a countable complete metric space has isolated points (in fact infinitely many if it is infinite), and $\mathbb R$ is uncountable.
  • Completion: every metric space sits densely in a complete one ($\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb R$).

5. Connectedness

  • Connected: not a union of two disjoint nonempty open sets. In $\mathbb R$ the connected sets are exactly the intervals.
  • Path connected $\Rightarrow$ connected. The converse fails: the topologist's sine curve $\{(x,\sin\frac1x):x>0\}$ together with points of $\{0\}\times[-1,1]$ is connected but not path connected. Open connected subsets of $\mathbb R^n$ are path connected.
  • Continuous images of connected sets are connected (IVT is the case of $\mathbb R$). A connected metric space with two points is uncountable ($x\mapsto d(a,x)$ hits an interval).
  • Closure: if $A$ is connected and $A\subseteq B\subseteq\overline A$, then $B$ is connected.
  • Components: maximal connected subsets; they are closed. Count them with continuous invariants: $\{(b,c):b^2\ne4c\}$ has two; $\{f:\int f\ne0\}$ in $C[0,1]$ has two; $\{A:AA^t=M\}$ for positive definite $M$ is a copy of $O(2)$, with two.
  • Graphs: the graph of a continuous function on an interval is connected (a solution set like $(x^3+1)^3=(y^5+1)^5$ is such a graph).
  • Removing sets: $\mathbb R^n$ minus a countable union of lines ($n\ge3$), or $\mathbb C^n$ minus a complex hypersurface, stays path connected; $\mathbb R$ minus a point does not.
  • Showing non-homeomorphic: count components after removing a point ($\mathbb R\not\cong\mathbb R^2$; a circle is not homeomorphic to an interval). There is no continuous bijection $\mathbb R\to[0,1]$.
