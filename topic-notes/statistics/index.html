Statistics

1. Methods of estimation

A statistic is a function of the sample; an estimator $\hat\theta$ is a statistic used to guess $\theta$.

Method of moments. Equate sample moments to population moments and solve: $\bar X=E_\theta[X]$, $\frac1n\sum X_i^2=E_\theta[X^2]$, …

Maximum likelihood. Maximise $L(\theta)=\prod f(x_i;\theta)$, usually via $\ell=\log L$ and $\ell'(\theta)=0$.

  • Check the support. When the support depends on $\theta$ (Uniform$(0,\theta)$, $e^{-(x-\theta)}$ for $x>\theta$), $L$ is maximised at an endpoint, e.g. $\hat\theta=X_{(n)}$ or $X_{(1)}$, not at a zero of the derivative.
  • Discrete parameter: compare $L(\theta+1)/L(\theta)$ with $1$, or take the largest allowed value.
  • Invariance: the MLE of $g(\theta)$ is $g(\hat\theta)$. (Median of Exp with mean $\mu$: $\hat\mu\log2=\bar X\log2$.)

Standard MLEs: Bernoulli $\hat p=\bar X$; Poisson $\hat\lambda=\bar X$; Exp (rate) $\hat\lambda=\frac1{\bar X}$; Normal $\hat\mu=\bar X$, $\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$; Uniform$(0,\theta)$ $\hat\theta=X_{(n)}$.

2. Properties of estimators

  • Unbiased: $E_\theta[\hat\theta]=\theta$. $S^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$; $\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$ is not. For $N(0,\sigma^2)$, $\frac1n\sum X_i^2$ is unbiased.
  • Mean squared error: $\text{MSE}=\operatorname{Var}+\text{bias}^2$.
  • Consistency: $\hat\theta_n\to\theta$ in probability. Unbiased with variance $\to0$ is enough.
  • Fisher information: $I(\theta)=E\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log f(X;\theta)\right)^2\right]=-E\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\log f\right]$, and $I_n=nI$.
  • Cramér–Rao bound: for unbiased $T$ of $g(\theta)$ (regular families), $\operatorname{Var}T\ge\frac{g'(\theta)^2}{nI(\theta)}$.

3. Sufficiency, completeness, UMVUE

  • Sufficient: $T$ is sufficient if the conditional law of the sample given $T$ does not depend on $\theta$.
  • Factorisation theorem: $T$ is sufficient iff $f(x;\theta)=g(T(x);\theta)\,h(x)$. Indicators of the support count: for Uniform$(\theta-1,\theta)$-type families the sufficient statistic is $(\min,\max)$ of the suitably shifted observations.
  • A one-to-one function of a sufficient statistic is sufficient.
  • Exponential family $f=h(x)c(\theta)e^{\eta(\theta)T(x)}$: $\sum T(X_i)$ is sufficient and (if $\eta$ ranges over an interval) complete. For $N(0,\sigma^2)$: $\sum X_i^2$.
  • Complete: $E_\theta[g(T)]=0$ for all $\theta$ forces $g(T)=0$. ($\sum X_i$ for $N(0,\sigma^2)$ is not complete: its mean is $0$ for every $\sigma$.)
  • Minimal sufficient: $T(x)=T(y)$ iff $\frac{f(x;\theta)}{f(y;\theta)}$ does not depend on $\theta$.
  • Ancillary: a statistic whose distribution does not depend on $\theta$. Basu: a complete sufficient statistic is independent of every ancillary statistic.
  • Rao–Blackwell: $E[W\mid T]$ for sufficient $T$ improves an unbiased $W$.
  • Lehmann–Scheffé: an unbiased function of a complete sufficient statistic is the unique UMVUE.

4. Confidence intervals

A $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval $(L,U)$ satisfies $P_\theta(L\le\theta\le U)=1-\alpha$ for every $\theta$.

  • Pivotal method: find $Q(X,\theta)$ whose distribution is free of $\theta$, take its quantiles, and solve for $\theta$.
  • Normal mean, $\sigma$ known: $\bar X\pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}$. Sample size for half-width $d$: $n=\left(\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\sigma}d\right)^2$ (with $z_{0.025}=1.96$).
  • Normal mean, $\sigma$ unknown: $\bar X\pm t_{n-1,\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}$. Variance: $\left(\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\chi^2_{n-1,\alpha/2}},\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\chi^2_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}}\right)$.
  • Exponential mean $\mu$: $\frac{2\sum X_i}{\mu}\sim\chi^2_{2n}$.
  • Location families like $e^{-(x-\theta)}$, $x>\theta$: $X_{(1)}-\theta\sim$ Exp$(n)$, so $P(X_{(1)}-\theta\le b)=1-e^{-nb}$ gives intervals $[X_{(1)}-b,X_{(1)}]$.

5. Hypothesis testing

  • Errors: type I $=$ rejecting a true $H_0$ (probability $\alpha$, the size); type II $=$ accepting a false $H_0$. Power $=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_1)$.
  • Test function: $\phi(x)\in[0,1]$ is the probability of rejecting; randomise on a boundary to hit the size exactly (needed for discrete data).
  • Neyman–Pearson lemma: for simple $H_0:f_0$ vs $H_1:f_1$, the most powerful size-$\alpha$ test rejects when $\frac{f_1(x)}{f_0(x)}>k$ (randomising when $=k$). In a table, order the points by $f_1/f_0$ and add them until the size is reached.
  • Monotone likelihood ratio: if $\frac{f(x;\theta_1)}{f(x;\theta_0)}$ increases in $T(x)$ for $\theta_1>\theta_0$, then "reject when $T>c$" is UMP for $H_0:\theta\le\theta_0$ vs $H_1:\theta>\theta_0$. (Exponential families with increasing $\eta$.)
  • Likelihood ratio test: reject when $\Lambda=\frac{\sup_{H_0}L}{\sup L}$ is small; $-2\log\Lambda\approx\chi^2_d$ for large $n$.
  • Standard tests: $z$-test (known $\sigma$), $t$-test (unknown $\sigma$), $\chi^2$-test for variance, $F$-test for two variances.
  • p-value: the probability, under $H_0$, of data at least as extreme as observed; reject when $p\le\alpha$.
